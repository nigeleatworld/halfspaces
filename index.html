01

Positioning research for Keynote Learning.

Delivered2026Client work

My first independent research client: a Singapore tuition centre that wanted to know which position it could credibly own. An online survey of 300 screened parents, a gap analysis, and a competitor scan, delivered as one recommendation the client could act on.

How I worked

Senior-direct, no agency layer. I designed the instrument, ran the analysis, and turned the findings into a clear recommendation. I used AI to accelerate production and quality checks; the research design, analysis, and interpretation were mine.

  • Importance is ranked, not rated. A forced top-3 ranking shows trade-offs instead of politeness.
  • Satisfaction is a net, not a mean. % like minus % dislike separates approval from friction.
  • The zones are relative. Median splits survive a skeptical CFO.
  • Small cells stay out. Any cut under n=30 is left out rather than dressed up.

Keynote’s findings stay with Keynote. The exhibit below is the same method run on a fictional brand, with synthetic data.

Kopi Lima, a fictional brand · importance vs. satisfaction

Speed and seating carry the widest gaps; taste is already a strength. The territory worth testing is the fast, reliable kopi run.

02

Reading personality from a cai fan order.

Research alpha, pausedcft.halfspaces.co

Everyone in Singapore has a cai fan ritual: the queue, the tray, the gravy call at the end. The Cai Fan Test is a behavioural instrument dressed as lunch, paired with an established Big Five questionnaire so its reads can be calibrated against real answers. Take it if you’re curious.

Where it stands

A working research alpha, fifteen builds in. Development is paused; the current alpha remains live. The latest build explains each read dish by dish, from what actually went on your tray. The twelve plate reads are provisional, and they stay that way until the calibration pilot says otherwise. Held to research standards from day one: separate consent, deletion codes, versioned scoring, low-base suppression.

03

A poker room that coaches you after the hand.

Playablehalfspaces.co/pokerspace

Pokerspace is offline Texas Hold’em in one file: a tournament, a cash table, and a career that climbs from a Freeroll to a 10,000 Main Event. Opponents get harder up the ladder, and the coaching waits until the hand is over, the way a player reviews a session. Where a decision cannot be graded fairly, it says so; raises are never graded.

How it was built

Started as a one-shot prompt in August, then finished in September and October through cloud coding sessions, with OpenAI’s Codex reviewing each pull request before it landed. That review caught maths that would have taught the wrong lesson more than once. The bots are credible recreational players, not a solver; the build journal says exactly what they do and do not read.

  • Coaching after the fact. Calls and folds are priced against the pot you can actually win, side pots included.
  • A ladder with stakes. Buy-ins, shootout brackets and real payout tables; in Career, nobody wears a label at the table.
  • Tested and benchmarked. 264 automated tests, and a benchmark of over a million hands behind the difficulty curve.

04

An iPhone app that teaches you to read Python.

On the App StoreiPhone and iPad2026

Bitpybit is Python practice for people who work with spreadsheets and data. Each case is a short script and a task: predict what it prints, compare that with what was actually asked, then choose the check worth running. The scripts go wrong the way real analysis goes wrong: a valid zero dropped as missing, the wrong denominator, a duplicate counted twice. Some are already correct, and the lesson is knowing that.

How it shipped

Built in SwiftUI with AI coding tools, and kept deliberately small: no account, no backend, lessons bundled on the device. Apple’s first answer was a request for more information, which is standard for a new developer account: a screen recording on a real phone and six questions. I answered, resubmitted, and version 1.0 was approved on 6 October.

  • Judgment, not syntax. 24 cases across 8 topics, three decisions each, with a guide when you need one and an explanation after every answer.
  • Private by default. Your answers and progress stay on your device; it works offline.
  • Free to start. The foundation questions and the first three cases are free; one payment unlocks the rest, with no subscription.
