Building the Cai Fan Test

A personality read from what you put on a cai fan tray, built as a research instrument. Fifteen versions in, paused, and still live.

July–September 2026 · built with Claude and Codex, each checking the other · alpha15 live at cft.halfspaces.co · development paused

Why lunch

Everyone in Singapore has a cai fan ritual: the queue, the pointing, the gravy call at the end. It’s a set of fast choices made under a little social pressure, with real trade-offs, which suits a behavioural measure.

The first prototype went up on 19 July. The next day the stallholder became the mechanism: the longer you take, the more impatient he gets, through his words and a restrained change in how he looks. There’s no countdown, no penalty and no forced choice; it just feels like a real queue. How long you were exposed to his impatience is recorded, and it never touches your score.

Alongside it sits an optional short Big Five questionnaire, an established one, so that one day the plate reads can be checked against real answers instead of my assumptions.

Every possible tray

By 23 July the menu had grown to 36 dishes across three counters, and you pick between two and four. Twelve reads came out of the scoring. To check they didn’t pile everyone into the same two or three, every valid tray was scored, gravy choices included: 1,333,500 of them. The reads are still labelled provisional, and they stay that way until a calibration study says otherwise.

The pilot broke the measurement

In August a few friends took it, and the pilot exposed a misunderstanding: the two-to-four dish limit could read as applying to each counter instead of the whole plate, so when the test made someone put dishes back, the data recorded it as a change of mind. One person never found the second and third counters at all.

So the data was recording confusion as if it were behaviour. Alpha14 fixed the wording, and moved the study to a new version number so that data would never be pooled with what came after.

One proposed fix was to group the dishes by type, vegetables in one place and meat in another. Codex advised against it, because grouping could steer people’s choices and suggest what a normal plate looks like. I agreed, and the dishes stayed ungrouped.

The share card

I wanted a result card people would post. Claude wanted to leave it for later; I pushed back, because you can’t measure whether people will share something that doesn’t exist yet, and it got built.

The first round went through Canva, and Codex called it “generic chinoiserie, not Singaporean”. I agreed. Canva also baked the text into the image, so nothing on it could change per person, and the card moved into code. I chose dish names over a photo, and “ZHUP” over “GRAVY”.

“Everything is stall paper”

The first screens looked like a hawker stall. The later ones quietly turned into a generic survey: rounded app-style cards for consent, plain browser form fields further on. I flagged it, Claude reviewed the whole journey separately, and the redesign got a four-word direction that every screen has followed since: everything is stall paper.

Eight agents, one folder

At one point eight AI coding processes were working in the same project at once, and they swept each other’s changes into unrelated commits. A guard that runs the tests before anything is committed limited the damage. Avoiding it meant giving each session its own working copy, or running one at a time. I still avoid running two sessions in the same project at once.

Research standards, as far as they go

By the pilot it had the basics a real study needs: separate consent for the research part, a deletion code so anyone can remove their answers, scoring that’s versioned so different versions never mix, and small groups kept out of any result. What it doesn’t have is validation. Nobody has shown yet that tray choices predict personality; in October a freshness check by Mr. B caught the homepage saying “calibrated”, and it now says “can be calibrated”. The counter photos and the stallholder are AI-generated.

What AI did, and what it didn’t

The code and the reviews were AI. Claude reviewed the scoring and the Big Five items and ran the design audit and release checks; Codex vetted the share card and the pilot fixes, and built alpha15; Grok, a third provider, gave alpha15 its final ship call. The idea, the ambition, the look and the words were mine, and so were recruiting the pilot and deciding to pause.

Paused, still live

At the end of September I paused development with alpha15 live. The latest version explains each read dish by dish, from what went on your tray. The next real step would be the calibration study, and I haven’t run it.
