Build journal

Building One Shot

A sniper game I built, argued about with two other AIs, and shelved; the interesting part is that they told me to throw away my best day’s work, and they were right.

July–August 2026 · built with Claude · reviewed independently by Codex and Grok · shelved at five scenarios

It started with someone else’s prompt

In late July a prompt did the rounds: build a first-person shooter at Call of Duty quality, fan out sub-agents, have a harsh critic compare it side by side with the real thing, loop until perfect. Matt Shumer published the result as Claude of Duty, and I asked Claude to digest it before I decided whether to copy the approach.

The useful thing was not in the prompt. It was in the repo’s own README, which almost nobody quotes: the fan-out approach the prompt advertises lost. Three rounds of six parallel agents moved the quality score by +0.46 and pushed defects up, 60 to 47 to 66. One sequential pass with a single owner per coupled concern moved it +1.00 and cut defects to 26. The lighting, sky and tonemapping were one system wearing three folder names, and isolated agents kept breaking each other’s assumptions.

So the famous prompt describes the method that underperformed, and the fix was discovered mid-build and written up honestly afterwards. I took the fix and left the prompt.

I built the thing I could measure

I did not want a Call of Duty clone; that target beat a fleet of agents and scored 5.05 out of 10. I wanted something with a bar I could actually check. So: a sniper trainer with Counter-Strike movement ported literally, real projectile ballistics, bullet drop, drag, a scope zeroed at 100 metres, breathing sway you steady by holding your breath.

Then I measured it, obsessively. Counter-strafing turned out to work without being implemented, falling straight out of a faithful port of Source’s acceleration function; the optimum stop is a seven-tick tap that halts you in 141 milliseconds against 359 for simply releasing the key. Drop at 180 metres is 17 units, enough to slide a chest shot onto the stomach. All verified, all reproducible.

Every number was right. None of the numbers could see that it was not a game.

Two other models, asked separately

I have a wrapper that sends work to OpenAI’s Codex, and I added one for OpenRouter so I could reach Grok. The rule I settled on: give each model the same work and never show it what the other said, because agreement only means something when it is independent.

Codex found that my movement basis was mirrored, so W walked the player backwards and A and D were swapped. My own tests had passed, because they measured speed, which is a scalar, and a scalar cannot fail a direction test. The instrument was structurally blind to the bug.

Grok found something Codex missed entirely, and it is the worst bug the project had: the heads-up display and the weapon disagreed about what “stopped” meant. The display said ACCURATE below 2.4 units per second; scoped, which is how the game is actually played, the weapon needed 1.08. The central readout of a trainer was lying in the exact stance you play in.

Then I asked them the question I had been avoiding: would anyone play this?

Both said no. Independently, and in nearly the same words. A tech demo with a sniper fetish, said one. The fantasy dies at first paint, before any ballistics work matters. Both proposed the same replacement, which was sitting in the project’s name the whole time: one bullet.

The day I made it worse

So I pivoted: five authored situations, one round each, a hard outcome. Then my client said it was boring and did not look real, which was fair, and I spent a day fixing that. A procedural town. A rooftop firing position. Physically based materials, ambient occlusion, bloom, filmic tone mapping, a real captured sky.

It looked considerably better and it played considerably worse.

The verdict from both reviewers, again independently, was to revert all of it. The grey box I had replaced was a deliberate value study; measured target-to-background contrast had fallen to 13, which is close to invisible. The rooftop broke sightlines the scenarios were authored for, and you could walk off the edge, which explained the “it’s panning all over the shop” complaint I had spent an hour misdiagnosing as a recoil problem.

I reverted the lot. A day’s work, deleted on the advice of two machines that had never seen each other’s reasoning.

The bugs were never in the physics

Two complaints arrived that sounded like broken hit detection: a missed shot scored as a hit, and a shot that visibly went through the figure scored as a miss.

Neither was a physics bug. The first was the bullet camera sitting fourteen units to the side of the round, so you were judging a three-dimensional line from a viewpoint that was not on that line; at 105 metres the parallax is easily enough to invert the verdict. The second was worse and funnier: the figure had two three-unit gaps between its torso and its arms, so rounds genuinely passed through the silhouette and hit nothing. I had introduced the holes myself, an hour earlier, splitting the chest into heart and lung.

When the player and the picture disagree, the picture is usually mine.

What survived

The bullet camera, which is the one thing my client actually enjoyed: the round is followed in roughly 27x slow motion, 86 per cent of the distance covered in the first third of the time and then a crawl, a dolly from 62 degrees to 36, a freeze on impact. Real flight time sets the pace, so the 400 metre shot takes visibly longer to arrive than the 100 metre one.

And a scoring formula that rewards the whole spectrum rather than pass or fail: placement earns a base, speed multiplies it, and a bad hit still banks something. A fast head shot is 1,500 points; a slow head shot is 826; a slow leg is 99. Zero for a poor shot is pass/fail wearing a number, and it gives someone who has already fumbled one scenario no reason to care about the next four.

What I am taking with me

Measure the construct, not the proxy. Every instrument I built asked whether the simulation was correct. The only question that mattered was whether it was fun, and nothing I built could see it. Anyone who has designed a survey knows this failure; it is easier to spot in someone else’s questionnaire than in your own test harness.

Independent beats numerous. I could have spun up five more Claudes and they would have agreed with me, because they reason from the same priors and share my blind spots. One genuinely different model found the bug my own tests could not represent. Convergence between models that cannot see each other is the strongest signal available short of a user.

Polish on the wrong layer is negative, not neutral. Not wasted; negative. I made it prettier and less playable, and I did it because prettier was the thing I could watch improving.

Check the instrument before you believe the finding. Five separate times I announced a bug that turned out to be a defect in my own test rather than in the code. The physics was right every time.

Shelved

Five scenarios, one bullet each, and both reviewers agree that exhausts quickly. Getting to an audience means twenty-odd scenarios and real art, which is weeks, and I have work that pays. So it stops here, at a thing that is genuinely playable and honestly small.

The workflow it produced is the part I kept.
